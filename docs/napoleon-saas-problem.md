# The Napoleon SaaS Problem

I keep coming back to the same observation: intelligence does not live only in the brain. It lives in the tools the brain can reach.

Look at Napoleon. The man believed he was the most capable strategist of his age, and in many ways he was. But the only tools he had were guns, the willingness of men to die for him, and horses. That was the entire substrate available for projecting power and coordinating resources. There was no way to grow the pie in any meaningful sense. Productivity gains were slow, capital was immobile, information moved at the speed of a courier, and the only reliable method of expansion was to take land, gold, and people from someone else. So of course the strategic imagination of that era stayed zero-sum. It was not a personal failure. It was a limitation of the tools. Poor guy, in a way. He was operating at the absolute ceiling of what his technology allowed.

Now look at an ordinary person in 2026. No crown. No army. Yet that person can fly across continents in a few hours, drive a machine that would have been pure magic to any 19th-century mind, eat food of higher quality and variety than most kings of the past, and maintain contact with more human beings in a single day than Napoleon could reach in a year. The cognitive hardware is roughly the same. The tools are not. The tools raised the effective intelligence and the possible output of the average human by orders of magnitude. The goals themselves changed shape because the substrate changed.

I apply the same lens to companies and I see the identical pattern.

Most companies today are not coherent organisms. They are collections of fragmented pieces held together by human effort. You store money in a bank. You take payments through Stripe. You manage expenses with Brex or something like it. You keep the books in QuickBooks. You track customers in Salesforce. You run marketing through HubSpot. You buy attention from Google and Meta. Each of these systems is excellent at its narrow job and almost completely blind to the others. The company itself has no single place where its full state can be seen, reasoned about, and acted upon.

So what do we do? We hire people to act as the glue. Intelligent, expensive human beings spend their days reconciling data across systems, chasing context that should already be shared, translating between tools that refuse to speak the same language, and performing work that exists only because the substrate is broken. These people are not primarily inventing. They are compensating for the fact that the company’s tools force a low-resolution, high-friction view of reality.

This is the Napoleon condition applied to organizations. The tools we currently use make certain kinds of growth easy and other kinds almost impossible. Because the internal model of the company is scattered, the organization defaults to extractive or incremental strategies: hire more glue, buy more point solutions, out-market the competitor who is suffering from the same fragmentation. The energy that should go into inventing new capabilities is consumed by the simple act of keeping the existing pieces from falling apart.

I believe this is not an inevitable state of affairs. It is a temporary limitation of the tools we have accepted. A company should be able to hold its entire relevant state in one coherent place — money, commitments, customers, knowledge, obligations, operations — so that the whole can be observed and acted upon without requiring a permanent layer of human intermediaries. When that becomes possible, the purpose of the company can shift. Coordination and bookkeeping stop being the main tax on human attention. Innovation becomes the primary activity again.
